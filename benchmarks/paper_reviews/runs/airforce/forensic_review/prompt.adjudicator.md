## Multivitamin Compliance Reduces Injuries of Female Recruits at Air Force Basic Training: A Randomized Controlled Cohort Study

Korey B. Kasper, MD, CAQSM *; Meredith Cockerell, MS,  ATC†; Clarissa Escamilla, MS, RDN, LD‡;

Richard Gonzalez, MDS, RDN, LD‡

## ABSTRACT

## Introduction:

Department of the Air Force basic military training (BMT) is the 7.5-week initial entry training for all enlisting U.S. Air Force and Space Force recruits. Overuse musculoskeletal injuries (MSKI) during training threaten success in BMT, and trainees with nutritional deficiencies are at higher risk. Several efforts are made at BMT to mitigate these issues to help trainees graduate on time, such as the distribution of multivitamins (MVI) to female recruits. However, trainee compliance with the prescribed MVI has been reported to be low, calling into question the effectiveness of this intervention. This study evaluated the effect of briefing modality offered to these trainees at the time of distribution on educational effectiveness, MVI compliance, and injury rates.

## Materials and Methods:

This was a randomized controlled cohort study of BMT female trainees. Subjects included all female trainees of a single iteration of BMT concurrently completing training within the same squadron. At the outset of training, cohorts were randomly educated in one of two ways regarding the provided MVI. The control cohort ( n = 80) was presented with the standard educational video created by BMT-registered dietitians, while the test cohort ( n = 79) was presented the video and then also briefed in-person by a group of subject matter experts. At the end of BMT, the trainees completed a questionnaire regarding their knowledge, confidence, and compliance with the MVI. Additionally, a routinely collected training database was queried and analyzed for differences in injuries and training outcomes using the chi-squared test with significance set to α≤ 0.05.

## Results:

Although both cohorts found their educational presentation useful, the 'in-person' cohort reported significantly higher compliance rates and were found to be diagnosed with MSKI and bone stress injuries at significantly lower rates (43% and 66% relative risk reductions, respectively).

## Conclusions:

Education to female BMT trainees regarding the MVI, which included a face-to-face discussion with subject matter experts,  resulted in greater compliance and reduced incidence of MSKI and bone stress injury, compared to a video prepared by registered dietitians alone. Such educational approaches should continue to be strived for in this and other military population health measures. This study's implication of MVI efficacy for injury risk reduction in female trainees should be further studied, verified, and improved upon in this and other populations.

## INTRODUCTION

Nutritional deficiency is common among military trainees 1-5 at  the  Department  of  the  Air  Force  basic  military training (BMT), the 7.5-week physically, mentally, and emotionally  demanding  initial  training  for  recruits  seeking enlistment  into  the  U.S.  Air  Force  and  U.S.  Space  Force. Deficiencies occurring before BMT (baseline nutrition) and during BMT (relative to training demands or in response to exercise and adaptations) can interfere with training success via effects on musculoskeletal injury (MSKI), 6 illness, 7-9 and poor mental and physical adaptation and performance. 4,10-19

Countless  questions  remain  regarding  the  effectiveness and efficacy of dietary supplementation for injury and performance  improvements, and  specifically  within  military trainees for improved operational outcomes. Despite the effort and resources appropriated for providing an MVI with iron to all female trainees, the intended benefits have not conclusively been demonstrated. 35 Issues with compliance as well as nutrient timing and dosage in relation to the high stresses of BMT may preclude realization of performance, injury, and attrition benefits. This study aimed to determine whether the addition of  an  in-person  brief  of  the  MVI  by  subject-matter  experts (SMEs)  at  the  time  of  distribution  would  improve  female BMT trainee knowledge, confidence, and compliance with the MVI and whether this, in turn, would affect MSKI/BSI incidence and training outcomes.  On the other hand,  improved compliance without a corresponding improvement of injury incidence or training outcomes could encourage BMT trainee health leadership to support discontinuation the MVI process at BMT for cost and time savings.

## METHODS

Every recruit arriving to BMT in the same week is assigned to the same 'squadron' for housing and training and are further divided into 'flights' (groups of ∼ 40 trainees of the same gender). Female trainees medically in-process as a unit with their flight  mates,  during which time they receive the MVI. The start date for a recruit is essentially random as is the flight to which they are assigned by BMT leadership.

All female trainees from a single training squadron who concurrently entered BMT in December 2021, and who had already been randomly assigned to 1 of the 4 flights,  were enrolled in this command-directed process improvement evaluation.  These  flights  were  randomly  assigned  to  cohorts, which  would  receive  one  of  two  MVI-briefing  modalities. The control cohort consisted of every recruit within 2 female trainee flights, which received standard MVI education via the BMT RD video with medical technician facilitation ('videoonly'  cohort).  The  test  cohort  consisted  of  every  recruit within  the  other  2  female  trainee  flights,  which  were  presented  the  same  video  and  reviewed  important  information with  an  in-person  team  of  SMEs  (the  trainee  health  clinic medical  director/sports  medicine  physician,  RD,  and  diet technician) and were given an opportunity to ask questions ('in-person' cohort). Because this study was designed as an internal,  commander-directed  process  improvement  evaluation, and obtaining individual consent from each member of the squadron was deemed overly burdensome and costly to the training and medical processing schedule, trainees were not made aware that their briefing experience was different from that of any others; this was further deemed crucial since trainee knowledge of their compliance being assessed would likely skew their compliance and report thereof.  Flight and squadron leadership were also not made aware of the change in  education,  nor  were  other  medical  providers.  No  other cohort differences with regards to MVI distribution,  education,  training,  or  medical  care  occurred  for  the  entirety  of BMT. Multiple  routinely  collected  and  manually  linked  (person  level,  via  full  name  and  DoD  identification  number) databases were queried for this study. The Electronic Basic Training Management System is maintained by Air Education and Training Command and stores demographic data such as squadron and flight assignment, age, gender (as identified in the Defense Enrollment Eligibility Reporting System), body mass index (BMI), and training outcome data (e.g., attrition and training delays). A database of MSKI diagnosed at BMT is  maintained  by  sports  medicine  staff,  which  is  routinely analyzed by members of the study team for injury trend monitoring and program improvement. Furthermore, all trainees completed questionnaires regarding their perceptions regarding the usefulness and effectiveness of their MVI briefing and were asked to quantify their MVI compliance. All data linkage and analysis (injury, training delays, graduation, briefing perceptions, and compliance) occurred following completion of the training period.

For this prospective, randomized controlled cohort study, a power analysis calculation was completed using α≤ 0.05 for the significance level, a power of 0.99. We estimated a 25% relatively greater daily MVI compliance in trainees receiving the in-person brief compared to those viewing the video alone, which yielded a need for each cohort to have 77 trainees to demonstrate a significant difference.

Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test. Significance was set to α≤ 0.05. Statistical  analyses  were  performed  using  SAS  version  9.4 (Statistical Analysis Software, Cary, NC).

## DISCUSSION

From  the  results  of  this  study,  we  infer  that  incorporating an in-person brief from SMEs improved compliance with the MVI which in turn significantly reduced injuries. The relative and absolute risk reductions for diagnosis with all MSKI were 43% and 13.5%,  respectively.  This  absolute  risk  reduction corresponds to a number needed to in-person brief (NNIPB) of 7.4 to avoid one MSKI. Said another way, with ∼ 40 female trainees in a briefing,  each in-person brief delivered would be expected to prevent 5.4 MSKI compared to the video-only brief. The relative and absolute risk reductions for diagnosis with BSI were 66% and 10%, respectively; this corresponds to  a  NNIPB  of  10.  Said  another  way,  each  in-person  brief (of  40  female  trainees)  would  be  expected  to  prevent  four BSIs.  Based  on  current  literature  on  nutritional  deficiency, dietary supplementation, and BSI risk, the iron 22,33 and vitamin D/calcium 28 content of the MVI is suspected to have been most beneficial to BSI reduction in this study. Though less is known regarding the association with injury and efficacy for risk reduction of other vitamins and minerals in the MVI, they may also have supported healthy tissue adaptations to training stress.

Although neither this prospective study nor the retrospective  study  by  Barnes  et al.  detected  significant  differences in training disruption, we showed a significant difference in BSI,  whereas  Barnes  et al.  did  not. 35 This  is  likely  due  to this study's randomized nature as opposed to using a historical,  season-matched comparison group as the control.  Our design controlled for temporal fluctuations in diagnostic patterns,  access  to  care,  care-seeking  behaviors,  and  training activities and policies, which may have skewed the findings of the prior study. The lack of significant difference in attrition despite differences in BSI, as was also the finding in a recent randomized controlled trial by Kasper et al., 24 is a testament to the early identification and efficient rehabilitation of MSKI provided by the BMT sports medicine team, which was implemented specifically to mitigate the operational impact of injury at BMT. 25

This study was limited in several ways: (1) it used block rather than individual randomization, (2) all trainees received the MVI (because of the potential benefits of the MVI), (3) it did not collect data regarding existing nutritional deficiencies, and (4) it was only conducted on a single BMT iteration so it cannot be known how the difference in season may affect the outcome. Despite these limitations, it adds to the body of knowledge regarding the benefits of this approach in female military trainees. Besides the presence and engagement of the SMEs at the briefing,  to  the  authors'  knowledge,  no  other differences in training or healthcare occurred between these two groups to explain the decrease in BSI. Flights within a squadron undergo nearly identical training, tissue loads, and access to medical care (the same embedded athletic trainers and sports medicine physicians), so it is unlikely that being in  different  flights  alone  would  account  for  the  differences in injury diagnoses. A 2022 study of the implementation of mass running gait training at BMT found that trainees who experienced a face-to-face encounter with medical personnel, who introduced themselves as available to help with issues related  to  BMT  completion,  reduced  hesitancy  to  be  seen, and  subsequently  increased  BSI  diagnoses. 38 With  a  similar interaction between trainees and embedded medical staff in  our  study  potentially  also  increasing  trainee  willingness to  report  symptoms,  resultant  injury  diagnoses  could  have been promoted among the in-person MVI-briefing cohort, 38 but  there  were  still  significantly  fewer  injury  diagnoses  in this cohort. Controlling for this possible confounder in future work may reveal a larger decrease in injuries attributable to MVI compliance alone.

Overweight and obesity is common in the U.S. Military (following training), and programs are in place throughout the DoD to address this. 39 However, it is important to remember the nutritionally related health and performance issues related to the underconsumption of nutrients within military training situations to fuel necessary performance and required physical adaptations 17 ; nutritional programs must be specific to the particular setting or goal (training vs. readiness maintenance). Interventions  aimed  at  overcoming  deficiencies  relative  to training and providing the timely delivery of relevant health and  performance  nutrition  education  and  interventions  are vital in improving success rates at BMT. Nutritional supplementation  in  military  training  should  be  further  studied  to verify its efficacy in injury reduction in at-risk populations and improve formulations specific to the needs of the population.

At  the  time  of  this  study, the  standard  was  to  brief trainees  on  the  MVI  via  video,  which  avoided  repetitively burdening  the  clinical  staff  with  being  present  for  multiple  briefings  each  week  at  various  times.  Although  simplification  and  automation  are  often  valuable  for  repetitive processes,  this study shows that an automated (video-only) MVI  briefing  at  BMT  was  associated  with  worse  compliance  and  injury  rates  than  an  in-person  briefing.  In-person medical  intervention  education  by  interested  SMEs,  albeit more burdensome, has demonstrable benefits on the learner's confidence in their understanding and compliance. The cost and  benefits  of  educational  modalities  regarding  individual or  population  health  measures  should  be  carefully  considered,  and whenever feasible and cost effective,  face-to-face educational  approaches  should  be  undertaken. Since  this study, embedded RDs have regularly delivered the BMT MVI briefings.

This  study  is  but  a  glimpse  of  the  benefits  to  military training  that  are  possible  with  improved  embedded  nutrition  services.  Future  research  should  evaluate  the  benefit of embedded nutrition assets at BMT on training outcomes. Fisher et al. 25 showed that embedding sports medicine services at BMT improved on-time graduation rates and reduced costs, time out of training, and attrition. Embedded nutritional medicine assets at BMT may be similarly beneficial by providing sports performance nutrition education, reducing barriers to nutrition care, and working toward improved DFAC offerings and environments. The efficient production of fit and healthy airmen at BMT prepared for military service would be best served by continuing to assess and improve trainee performance health education regarding balanced, healthy diets that meet the nutritional needs of their diverse lifestyles and evolving careers.

## CONCLUSION

This  study  shows  that  including  face-to-face  education  to female BMT trainees regarding a MVI by SMEs resulted in greater  compliance  and  reduced  MSKI  and  BSI  incidence, compared to a video prepared by RDs alone. Such educational approaches should continue to be strived for in this and other military population health measures. This study's implication of MVI efficacy for injury risk reduction in female military trainees  should  be  further  studied,  verified,  and  improved upon in this and other populations.
